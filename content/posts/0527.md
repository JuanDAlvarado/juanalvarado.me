## On Film
My first ever camera was a 1973 Yashica Electro GSN. I found it in a Goodwill bin with the factory leather case. In the next bin down, I found the original lens screw-on accessories. I paid about $15 for the whole set. It was in pristine exterior condition, the lens attachments were even in their original plastic wrap within the leather holder. The only issue I could find was that the whole thing smelled like it had been hot boxed by cheap cigarettes for the last 40 years. As I was wiping it down with towels and water, a thick yellow layer came off that smelled **gross**. The strap, the leather wrap, the satin chrome finish, every part was wiped down then left in the sun for a few hours to let the UV do some magic. Eventually it didn't smell like a smoker's toothbrush and I loaded some film into it. The first roll came back riddled with accidental double exposures and feed issues. Despite running into the Yashica's dreaded Pad of Death issue (a well documented report of this by the Yashica guy [here](http://www.yashica-guy.com/document/repair.html#nine)), the black and white images of that first roll were nothing short of magical to me. Little did I know that I had just unlocked my next obsession. 

Most people start with a DSLR or even a point and shoot before going into the 35mm film world. I did not grow up with a ton of disposable income that would've allowed my parents to indulge me. I didn't even have a major interest in the world of photography prior to discovering this relic in the bins at Goodwill. One thing led to another and I had a desire to try out an SLR camera, the Yashica being a Rangefinder. So after a quick look on Facebook Marketplace, I found a treasure, a 1984 Canon AE-1 Program with a full array of lenses to try out. It came with the standard 50mm f/1.8 lens, as well as a 70-210mm, with a Vivitar 28-90mm lens bundled in. This all came out to about $150 at the time of purchase. This camera ended up being (and still is) my most beloved. I got the rolls back and would be shooting whatever I could find. 

My absolute favorite thing about film photography is the way that it actually captures the scene. A typical digital camera like your phone will use a sensor that attempts to reproduce the light level and color that hits the pixel that corresponds to the surface of the sensor. It can then be manipulated to receive or output more or less of what you saw then. It is a representation, a digital recreation, it can calculate and measure the exact amount of light that was present. Film not only records what the light actually did, but it creates a kind of fossil of that exact moment.

The actual roll of film is made up of a plastic base like cellulose acetate or polyester coated with an emulsion of gelatin infused with silver halide crystals. These chemicals are photosensitive due to the silver halides, usually silver bromide or silver chloride, and when you expose a picture, depending on your focus, your aperture (the size of the iris on the lens), your exposure time, and your film's light sensitivity rating (the ISO), the silver particles in the film snap into place. They drink up the light and color for the amount of time your shutter speed is set to (often a fraction of a second) and then freeze once they are in complete darkness. They, like our eyes, took in the scene as it was, and recreate it as it was in that very moment. When I'm holding the negatives in my hand, I am holding not just a photo, but the very certificate of existence in that moment, of the light in that place as my camera and my eyes perceived it. It is an artifact that proclaims "I was here and I saw this, and you can't deny it." The silver particles are present in the grains that the photo shows. The graininess is a unique fingerprint for a moment in time.

That artifact, the actual negatives, has been on my mind a lot recently. I work in the tech world, and my current workload is all about AI. I know firsthand just how extraordinary AI generation is getting these days. Anthropic's [Mythos](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/04/22/technology/anthropics-mythos-ai.html) model is so powerful that they decided that the world is simply not ready for that threshold to be crossed, and decided not to release it publicly. Image generation models are equally as scary. OpenAI's [Images 2.0](https://openai.com/index/introducing-chatgpt-images-2-0/) displays some of the most convincing examples of AI image generation I've seen. With fewer mistakes and tells, AI generated images are terrifyingly close to being undetectable. Nowadays someone who has never left their own zip code can generate a convincing photo of them standing in front of the Mona Lisa. A person can pass off that they visited the Grand Canyon with perfectly realistic lighting and never feel the gut dropping sensation you feel when seeing it in real life for the first time. The requirement of experience and reality is simply not there for a photograph today.

But I have the negatives. I have the proof. I may not ever hold my sister's hand again in this lifetime, but I can hold the photograph she captured of me on that day with my Yashica Electro 35. It may have double-exposed accidentally, the framing may not be ideal, focus may not be perfect, but none of that matters to me. I hold a certificate of her existence. No amount of technical prowess or seamless generation will ever top that. And so, I will continue attempting to capture some of my life's most important moments on film as often as I can. The silver halide was there, and so was she.
